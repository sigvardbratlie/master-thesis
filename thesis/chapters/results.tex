\section{Expected Performance Patterns}
% -------------------------------------------------------

Before presenting the empirical results, this section outlines the a priori expectations
that motivate the experimental design. These expectations are grounded in the architectural
differences between the two agent configurations and in the nature of legal case progression.

\paragraph{Quality metrics over session progression.}
The central hypothesis is that the performance trajectories of the two architectures diverge
as a case progresses. In the earliest sessions, the document set is small and the total
context volume is modest. Under these conditions the less complex agent --- which does a
simple retrieval based on a query --- performs well due to the manageable context size,
as the complexity of the task is low. The difference between the two architectures is
expected to be minimal.

As subsequent sessions introduce new documents, new parties, additional claims, and a
growing timeline, the situation reverses. The simpler RAG approach becomes increasingly
more difficult as the returned content does not necessarily contain sufficient context to
answer questions where the answer lies spread across multiple documents. The custom agent,
by contrast, maintains a continuously updated factsheet and performs targeted retrieval
against a variety of tools and structured information, as well as the same vector store
as the baseline, allowing it to leverage accumulated case knowledge rather than being
overwhelmed by it. The expected result is a positive trajectory for the custom agent
across sessions and a flat or degrading trajectory for the baseline.

\paragraph{Token usage over session progression.}
Token usage is expected to grow for both architectures as the case accumulates material,
but for structurally different reasons. For the baseline agent, input tokens often
increase with case size, as retrieval draws from different sources and accumulates over
time as many less relevant chunks are returned. For the custom agent, input token growth
reflects increasing \emph{richness} rather than raw volume: the factsheet grows denser,
but the returned context is compressed and the model is given a variety of options to
filter only the necessary. Output tokens are likewise expected to grow over time for the
custom agent, as richer context enables more thorough answers --- a positive signal rather
than an efficiency cost.

\paragraph{Inference time.}
Response latency is expected to increase with case progression for both architectures.

\subsection{DeepEval: Correctness, Relevancy, and Completeness}

The joint distribution of correctness, relevancy, and completeness across all queries
and sessions is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:deepeval-pairplot}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/deep_eval_pairplot.png}
    \caption{Pairplot of DeepEval quality metrics (correctness, relevancy, completeness).
             Diagonal panels show per-metric density estimates. The custom agent (orange)
             exhibits higher correctness and completeness, while relevancy is similarly
             high for both configurations.}
    \label{fig:deepeval-pairplot}
\end{figure}

The custom agent's correctness distribution is clearly left-skewed, with density
concentrated near 1.0, whereas the baseline distribution is considerably more spread.
This confirms that the correctness advantage is not driven by a small number of
high-scoring queries but reflects a broad shift in the distribution. Relevancy scores
are highly concentrated near 1.0 for both configurations --- as long as an agent attempts
to answer the question, the response tends to be on-topic regardless of architecture.
The low-scoring tail for the baseline corresponds primarily to cases where retrieval
fails entirely and no substantive response is produced. Completeness follows a similar
pattern to correctness: the custom agent's distribution skews higher, while the baseline
exhibits more low-completeness responses. Overall, the pairplot reveals greater
consistency and reliability in the custom agent's responses across all three quality
dimensions, while the baseline's performance is more variable and more often falls short.

\bigbreak

Session-level trajectories tell the same story longitudinally (Figure~\ref{fig:deepeval-lineplot}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/deep_eval_lineplot.png}
    \caption{DeepEval quality metrics across sessions for each agent configuration.}
    \label{fig:deepeval-lineplot}
\end{figure}

Both agents broadly track each other through the session progression, but the custom
agent remains consistently above the baseline throughout. Crucially, the variance of
the custom agent's trajectory is substantially lower: where the baseline exhibits
wider swings between sessions, the custom agent maintains a more stable performance
profile. This session-level consistency reinforces the pairplot finding that the
quality advantage is broad and reliable rather than driven by occasional strong queries.

\bigbreak

Breaking the correctness trajectory down by dataset (Figure~\ref{fig:lineplot-correctness-by-dataset})
reveals that the performance gap is not uniform across cases.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/lineplot_correctness_by_dataset.png}
    \caption{Correctness over session progression, split by dataset.}
    \label{fig:lineplot-correctness-by-dataset}
\end{figure}

On TOSL-2024 --- the fully authentic case with the larger document volume and greater
factual complexity --- the custom agent's advantage grows more noticeably as sessions
accumulate. On THRD-2021, the custom agent also leads, but the gap is smaller and more
stable. This pattern is consistent with the architectural hypothesis: the factsheet and
structured retrieval tools confer the greatest benefit precisely when the case is large
and complex, where naive RAG retrieval is most prone to missing relevant facts dispersed
across many documents.

\bigbreak

The model-level breakdown (Figure~\ref{fig:deepeval-barplot-llms}) adds a further dimension.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/deep_eval_barplot_llms.png}
    \caption{DeepEval quality metrics broken down by underlying LLM. The custom agent
             outperforms the baseline for every model tested, with the gap narrowing for
             the most capable model.}
    \label{fig:deepeval-barplot-llms}
\end{figure}

The custom agent outperforms the baseline across all three metrics for every model
tested, with one exception: for the most capable model in the evaluation, the two
configurations converge and the difference becomes negligible. For the smaller and
less capable models, the margin is wider. This suggests that structured factsheet
context acts as a capability amplifier whose marginal effect is greatest when the
base model has limited capacity to compensate for noisy or incomplete retrieval.
The implications of this model-dependent effect are discussed further in
Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.

\subsection{BERTScore}
% -------------------------------------------------------

BERTScore measures surface-level semantic similarity to the reference answer and is
therefore less sensitive to structured factual completeness than the DeepEval metrics.
A response can score well on BERTScore while omitting specific facts that correctness
and completeness evaluation would penalise.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/bert_pairplot.png}
    \caption{Pairplot of BERTScore sub-metrics (precision, recall, F1, and sentence-BERT
             similarity). Distributions for the two agent configurations largely overlap,
             indicating comparable surface-level semantic quality.}
    \label{fig:bert-pairplot}
\end{figure}

The distributions have considerable overlap between the two agent types, but the custom
agent shows a higher peak with less variation and more consistent surface-level semantic
similarity to the reference answers. The baseline has a wider spread with more
low-scoring outliers, suggesting it occasionally produces responses less semantically
aligned with the reference. The near-identical BERTScore distributions complement rather
than contradict the DeepEval findings: both agents produce semantically coherent output,
but the custom agent more reliably includes the specific factual content the gold-standard
reference requires.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/bert_lineplot.png}
    \caption{BERTScore F1 and sentence-BERT similarity across sessions. Trajectories are
             flat and closely aligned between configurations, with the custom agent showing
             marginally narrower confidence intervals.}
    \label{fig:bert-lineplot}
\end{figure}

Across sessions, BERTScore F1 and sentence-BERT similarity are relatively flat and
closely aligned for both agent types, with only minor fluctuations. This is expected:
surface similarity to reference answers does not change systematically as the case grows,
since the phrasing of correct responses does not become more or less reference-like with
document volume. The session-level divergence observed in the DeepEval metrics is
therefore not an artefact of reference phrasing but reflects genuine differences in
factual coverage.

\subsection{Resource Metrics}
% -------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/resource_pairplot.png}
    \caption{Pairplot of resource metrics (duration in seconds, input tokens, output tokens,
             total tokens, and LLM call count). The custom agent exhibits higher input
             token usage reflecting factsheet growth, while inference duration is more
             tightly bounded than the baseline.}
    \label{fig:resource-pairplot}
\end{figure}

The most notable pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:resource-pairplot} is in input token usage.
The custom agent spans a wider range, with some queries consuming upwards of
200{,}000 input tokens --- consistent with the factsheet growing denser as the case
accumulates material and vector retrieval returning richer chunks. The baseline clusters
at lower input token counts, reflecting its simpler retrieval strategy, though it too
exhibits high-token outliers for sessions with large document sets.
Output tokens are similarly distributed, with the custom agent showing a slightly wider
tail toward higher values, in line with the expectation that richer context enables more
detailed answers. Inference duration is generally lower and more tightly concentrated
for the custom agent; the baseline exhibits heavier tails at high latency, likely due
to its unbounded document ingestion strategy. LLM call counts are comparable between
configurations, typically in the range of 1--3 calls per query.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/resource_lineplot.png}
    \caption{Resource metrics across sessions. Token counts and latencies fluctuate
             without a systematic trend for either configuration, and the two track each
             other closely throughout.}
    \label{fig:resource-lineplot}
\end{figure}

Unlike the quality metrics, no consistent trend is visible in the resource trajectories
over session progression. Token counts and latencies fluctuate without a systematic
increase, and the two configurations track each other closely throughout
(Figure~\ref{fig:resource-lineplot}). The absence of a growth trend is partly explained
by the context compression mechanisms applied to the custom agent: as the factsheet grows,
significance filtering and display shortening keep the payload from expanding
proportionally.

\section{Statistical Modeling}
% -------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Mann-Whitney U Test}

All hypothesis tests use the one-sided Mann-Whitney U test, appropriate given the
non-normal distributions of all metrics (Shapiro-Wilk, $p < 10^{-18}$ for every metric
tested). The null hypothesis in each case is that the custom agent's scores are not
stochastically greater than those of the baseline agent.

\paragraph{Quality metrics.}
Table~\ref{tab:mann-whitney} summarises the results. Correctness and relevancy both show
statistically significant advantages for the custom agent at the $\alpha = 0.05$ level.
Completeness does not reach significance, though the directional trend is the same.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Mann-Whitney U test results for quality metrics (one-sided, $H_1$: custom $>$ baseline).}
\label{tab:mann-whitney}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\hline
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{U-statistic} & \textbf{p-value} & \textbf{Result} \\
\hline
Correctness  & 105{,}587.00 & $8.49 \times 10^{-3}$ & Significant ($p < 0.05$) \\
Relevancy    & 101{,}782.50 & $3.42 \times 10^{-4}$ & Significant ($p < 0.05$) \\
Completeness &  38{,}820.00 & $8.34 \times 10^{-2}$ & Not significant \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The relevancy result warrants a cautious interpretation. Relevancy is almost universally
high for both agents whenever a response is attempted: as long as the agent engages with
the question, the answer tends to be on-topic regardless of architecture. The significant
difference is primarily driven by cases where the baseline fails to retrieve any relevant
documents and consequently produces no substantive answer, incurring a near-zero
relevancy score. \textbf{Correctness is the most diagnostic quality metric}: it is
hardest to satisfy and most sensitive to the architectural differences under evaluation,
and the significant result ($p = 8.49 \times 10^{-3}$) carries the most interpretive
weight.

The absence of a significant completeness result likely reflects the smaller number of
completeness observations in the dataset and the inherent difficulty of distinguishing
partial from complete answers when both agents frequently cover the required facts.

\paragraph{Resource metrics.}
Neither input token usage ($U = 99{,}750$, $p = 0.730$) nor inference duration
($U = 93{,}705$, $p = 0.163$) differs significantly between configurations. Mean input
token counts are almost identical (custom: 72{,}286 $\pm$ 77{,}114; baseline:
71{,}968 $\pm$ 75{,}645) and mean durations are within one second of each other
(custom: 35.1 s; baseline: 36.1 s). The custom agent achieves its quality improvements
without imposing a measurable additional cost in tokens or latency.

\section{Summary by Research Question}
\label{sec:rq-summary}
% -------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:rq-summary} summarises the empirical evidence for each research question.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Research question outcomes based on empirical results.}
\label{tab:rq-summary}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.08\textwidth} p{0.45\textwidth} p{0.35\textwidth}}
\hline
\textbf{RQ} & \textbf{Finding} & \textbf{Evidence} \\
\hline
Main & Custom agent improves factual accuracy without significant cost increase &
  Correctness significant ($p{=}8.5{\times}10^{-3}$); token usage and latency
  statistically equivalent \\[6pt]
RQ1 & Structured extraction mitigates cross-document limitations; effect is largest on
  the more complex, fully authentic dataset &
  Wider correctness gap on TOSL-2024; performance gap grows with case complexity \\[6pt]
RQ2 & Custom agent maintains stable or improving quality across sessions; baseline
  performance is more variable and lower throughout &
  Session trajectory plots; narrower confidence intervals and higher median for custom
  agent at later sessions \\[6pt]
RQ3 & Structured context amplifies smaller model performance; the gap between custom
  and baseline agents narrows as model capability increases &
  LLM barplot: gap is widest for smaller models, negligible for the most capable model \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Main RQ.}
The results support an affirmative answer: the custom agent achieves statistically
significant improvements in correctness and relevancy over the RAG-only baseline, while
consuming a statistically equivalent number of tokens and producing responses within the
same latency envelope. The quality gain is not purchased at a proportional resource cost.

\paragraph{RQ1.}
The evidence supports the claim that structured extraction mitigates cross-document
retrieval limitations. The performance advantage is more pronounced on TOSL-2024 ---
the larger, more complex, fully authentic case --- where queries more frequently require
synthesising evidence across many documents. On the simpler THRD-2021 dataset, the gap
is smaller, consistent with the theoretical expectation that the factsheet provides the
most benefit precisely when naive retrieval is most likely to fail.

\paragraph{RQ2.}
Session-level trajectories show that the custom agent maintains quality across all
sessions with narrower variance, while the baseline exhibits wider swings and a less
stable profile. This supports the conclusion that the structured representation scales
with document volume rather than degrading under it.

\paragraph{RQ3.}
The model-level breakdown provides the clearest evidence for RQ3: the custom agent's
structured context delivers the largest marginal gain for smaller and less capable
models, with the gap shrinking to near-zero for the most advanced model tested. This
suggests that the factsheet acts as a capability compensator --- partially substituting
for the reasoning capacity that larger models supply natively.
