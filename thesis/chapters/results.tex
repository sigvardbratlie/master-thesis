\section{Expected Performance Patterns}
% -------------------------------------------------------

Before presenting the empirical results, this section outlines the a priori expectations
that motivate the experimental design. These expectations are grounded in the architectural
differences between the two agent configurations and in the nature of legal case progression.

\paragraph{Quality metrics over session progression.}
The central hypothesis is that the performance trajectories of the two architectures diverge
as a case progresses. In the earliest sessions, the document set is small and the total
context volume is modest. Under these conditions the less complex agent—which does a simple 
retrieval based on a query—performs well due to the manageable context size, as the complexity
of the task is low. The difference between the two architectures is expected to be minimal.

As subsequent sessions introduce new documents, new parties, additional claims, and a
growing timeline, the situation reverses. The simpler RAG appraoch becomes increasinlgy 
more difficult as the returned content not neccesarily contains sufficient context to 
answer the question where the answer lies spread accross multiple documents. The agent is forced to ingest
an ever-larger payload, increasing the risk of retrieval confusion, cross-document
interference, and eventual context overflow or to answer on a poor ground, reducing the overall performance. 
The custom agent, by contrast, maintains a continuously updated factsheet and performs targeted retrieval 
against a varaity of tools and strucutred information, as well as the same vectorstore as the baseline, 
allowing it to leverage accumulated case knowledge rather than being overwhelmed by it. 
The expected result is a positive trajectory for the custom agent across sessions
and a flat or degrading trajectory for the baseline.

\paragraph{Token usage over session progression.}
Token usage is expected to grow for both architectures as the case accumulates material,
but for structurally different reasons. For the baseline agent, the number of input token often 
increases with the case size, as the retrieval search requires context from different sources and 
accumualtes over time when a lot of quires are done and each returns multiple chunks of whom many are less relevant. 
For the custom agent, input token growth reflects increasing
\emph{richness} rather than raw volume: the factsheet grows denser, the returnes context are compressed and the model 
are giving a variaty of options to filter only the neceacaccry. Output tokens are likewise expected to grow over time for the custom agent, as
richer context enables more thorough and nuanced answers. This growth is therefore a
positive signal—evidence that the agent is leveraging accumulated case knowledge—rather
than an efficiency cost.

\paragraph{Inference time.}
Response latency is expected to increase with case progression for both architectures.

Figure~\ref{fig:deepeval-pairplot} shows the joint distribution of correctness, relevancy,
and completeness across all queries and sessions, aggregated for each agent type.

The custom agent's correctness distribution (orange) is clearly left-skewed, with density
concentrated near 1.0, whereas the baseline distribution (blue) is considerably more
spread. This confirms that the correctness advantage observed in the longitudinal plot 
is not driven by a small number of high-scoring queries but reflects a broad shift in the distribution.

Relevancy scores are highly concentrated near 1.0 for both configurations. As long as an agent attempts to answer the question, relevancy is almost always rated high; the low-scoring tail for the baseline corresponds primarily to cases where retrieval fails entirely and no substantive response is produced. Correctness is therefore the more discriminating metric: it is harder to satisfy and captures differences in factual accuracy that relevancy is insensitive to.

Completeness shows a similar pattern to correctness: the custom agent's distribution skews
higher, while the baseline exhibits more low-completeness responses. This is consistent
with the expectation that the factsheet provides richer context for constructing
comprehensive answers, especially as the case grows in scope.

Overall, the pairplot reveals the increase in consistency and reliability of the custom agent's responses across all three quality dimensions, 
while the baseline's performance is more variable and often falls short.
\bigbreak

Figure~\ref{fig:deepeval-lineplot} shows the trajectories of the three DeepEval metrics across sessions. Both agents broadly track each other through the session progression, but the custom agent remains consistently above the baseline throughout. Crucially, the variance of the custom agent's trajectory is substantially lower: where the baseline exhibits wider swings between sessions, the custom agent maintains a more stable performance profile. This session-level consistency reinforces the pairplot finding that the quality advantage is not driven by occasional strong queries but reflects a broad, reliable improvement.

\bigbreak

Figure~\ref{fig:lineplot-correctness-by-dataset} breaks down the correctness trajectory by dataset. The two datasets have distinct characteristics: TOSL-2024 is a fully authentic case with a larger document volume and greater factual complexity, while THRD-2021 contains a mix of authentic court documents and reconstructed case materials (see Section~\ref{sec:dataset-limitations}). The dataset breakdown reveals that the performance gap between the two agents is more pronounced on TOSL-2024, where the custom agent's advantage grows more noticeably as sessions accumulate. On THRD-2021, the custom agent also leads, but the gap is smaller and more stable. This pattern is consistent with the architectural hypothesis: the factsheet and structured retrieval tools confer the greatest benefit precisely when the case is large and complex, where naive RAG retrieval is most prone to missing relevant facts dispersed across many documents.

\bigbreak

Figure~\ref{fig:deepeval-barplot-llms} shows the distribution of DeepEval metrics broken down by the underlying LLM. The custom agent outperforms the baseline across all three metrics for every model tested, with one exception: for Gemini 2.5 Pro — the most capable model in the evaluation — the two configurations converge and the difference becomes negligible. For the smaller and less capable models, the margin is wider. This pattern suggests that the structured factsheet context acts as a capability amplifier that has the greatest marginal effect when the base model has limited capacity to compensate for noisy or incomplete retrieval. The implications of this model-dependent effect are discussed further in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.


\subsubsection*{BERTScore}

Figure~\ref{fig:bert-pairplot} shows the joint distribution of BERTScore components
(precision, recall, F1, and sentence-BERT similarity). The distributions have considerable overlap between the two agent types, 
but the custom (orange) shows a higher peak, indicating less variation and more consistent surface-level semantic similarity to the reference answers. 
The baseline (blue) has a wider spread, with more low-scoring outliers, suggesting that it occasionally produces responses that are less semantically aligned with the reference.

This result is not unexpected. BERTScore measures surface-level semantic similarity to the
reference answer and is therefore less sensitive to structured factual completeness than
the DeepEval metrics. A response can score well on BERTScore while omitting specific facts
that correctness and completeness evaluation would penalise. The near-identical BERTScore
distributions therefore complement rather than contradict the DeepEval findings: both
agents produce semantically coherent output, but the custom agent more reliably includes
the specific factual content required by the gold-standard reference.

\bigbreak

Figure~\ref{fig:bert-lineplot} shows the trajectories of BERTScore F1 and sentence-BERT similarity across sessions.
The trajectories for both metrics are relatively flat and closely aligned between the two agent types, with only minor fluctuations. 
The custom agent (orange) shows a more consistent trajectory with narrower confidence intervals.

\subsection{Resource Metrics}
% -------------------------------------------------------

Figure~\ref{fig:resource-pairplot} shows the joint distribution of resource consumption
metrics: inference duration, input tokens, output tokens, total tokens, and number of LLM
calls per query.

The most notable pattern is in input token usage. The custom agent (orange) spans a wider
range, with some queries consuming upwards of 200{,}000 input tokens. This is consistent
with the expectation that the factsheet—injected in full on each call—grows denser as the
case accumulates material, and that the vector retrieval step returns richer and more
numerous chunks. The baseline agent (blue) clusters at lower input token counts, reflecting
its simpler retrieval strategy, though it too exhibits high-token outliers corresponding to
sessions with large document sets.

Output tokens are similarly distributed, with the custom agent showing a slightly wider
tail toward higher values. This is in line with the expectation that a richer context
enables more detailed answers.

Inference duration is generally lower and more tightly concentrated for the custom agent,
with the baseline exhibiting heavier tails at high latency. The structured retrieval
mechanism of the custom agent—bounded by configurable top-$k$ limits—appears to reduce
the variance in response time compared to the baseline's unbounded document ingestion.

LLM call counts are comparable between the two configurations, typically in the range of
1--3 calls per query, indicating that neither architecture requires substantially more
agentic steps than the other to produce a response.

\bigbreak
Figure~\ref{fig:resource-lineplot} shows the trajectories of resource metrics across sessions. Unlike the quality metrics, no consistent trend is visible for either agent over the session progression: token counts and latencies fluctuate without a systematic increase, and the two configurations track each other closely throughout. This is consistent with the statistical finding of no significant difference in token usage or inference time. The absence of a growth trend is partly explained by the context compression mechanisms applied to the custom agent's structured context: as the factsheet grows, significance filtering and display shortening keep the payload from expanding proportionally.

\section{Statistical Modeling}
\subsection{Mann-Whitney U Test}

As described in the evaluation design, all hypothesis tests use the one-sided Mann-Whitney U test, which is appropriate given the non-normal distributions of all metrics (Shapiro-Wilk, $p < 10^{-18}$ for every metric tested). The null hypothesis in each case is that the custom agent's scores are not stochastically greater than those of the baseline agent ($H_0: \eta_{\text{custom}} \leq \eta_{\text{baseline+RAG}}$).

\paragraph{Quality metrics.}
The results are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:mann-whitney}. Correctness and relevancy both show statistically significant advantages for the custom agent at the $\alpha = 0.05$ level. Completeness does not reach significance, although the directional trend is the same.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Mann-Whitney U test results for quality metrics (one-sided, $H_1$: custom $>$ baseline).}
\label{tab:mann-whitney}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\hline
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{U-statistic} & \textbf{p-value} & \textbf{Result} \\
\hline
Correctness  & 105{,}587.00 & $8.49 \times 10^{-3}$ & Significant ($p < 0.05$) \\
Relevancy    & 101{,}782.50 & $3.42 \times 10^{-4}$ & Significant ($p < 0.05$) \\
Completeness &  38{,}820.00 & $8.34 \times 10^{-2}$ & Not significant \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The relevancy result warrants a cautious interpretation. Relevancy is an almost universally high score for both agents whenever a response is attempted: as long as the agent engages with the question, the answer tends to be on-topic regardless of architecture. The performance gap on this metric therefore does not reflect finer differences in response targeting; it is primarily driven by cases where the baseline agent fails to retrieve any relevant documents and consequently fails to produce a substantive answer at all, incurring a near-zero relevancy score. In this sense, relevancy captures similar information to surface-level semantic similarity metrics such as BERTScore and sentence-BERT similarity — it is sensitive to retrieval failures but not to differences in factual depth. \textbf{Correctness is the most diagnostic quality metric}: it is the hardest to satisfy and the most sensitive to the architectural differences under evaluation, and the significant result there ($p = 8.49 \times 10^{-3}$) carries the most interpretive weight.

The absence of a significant completeness result likely reflects the smaller number of completeness observations in the dataset (completeness evaluation was not collected for all records) and the inherent difficulty of distinguishing partial from complete answers when both agents frequently cover the required facts.

\paragraph{Resource metrics.}
Testing the directional hypothesis that the custom agent consumes fewer resources than the baseline ($H_1$: custom $<$ baseline), neither input token usage ($U = 99{,}750$, $p = 0.730$) nor inference duration ($U = 93{,}705$, $p = 0.163$) differs significantly between configurations. Numerically, mean input token counts are almost identical (custom: 72{,}286 $\pm$ 77{,}114; baseline: 71{,}968 $\pm$ 75{,}645) and mean durations are within one second of each other (custom: 35.1 s; baseline: 36.1 s).

This result has an important practical implication: the custom agent achieves its quality improvements without imposing a measurable additional cost in tokens or latency. The context compression mechanisms described in Section~\ref{sec:design-choices} — filtering by significance and date range via database RPC functions, and display-shortening of structured elements — appear to offset the additional context introduced by the factsheet, keeping total token consumption comparable to the simpler retrieval approach.
